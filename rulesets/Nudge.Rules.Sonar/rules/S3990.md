# S3990 — Assemblies should be marked as CLS compliant

## Why
The `CLSCompliant` attribute declares whether your public API sticks to the
Common Language Specification subset usable from any .NET language (no
unsigned types in signatures, no case-only overload differences, etc.).
Without the declaration, consumers in other languages (F#, VB) can't tell
if your library is safe to call — and without the compiler check the
attribute enables, violations creep into public signatures unnoticed.

## Do this
1. Add `[assembly: CLSCompliant(true)]` to `AssemblyInfo.cs` (or the
   project-level assembly attributes file).
2. Fix the compiler warnings it surfaces: change public unsigned-type
   parameters to CLS-compliant alternatives, or mark the specific member
   `[CLSCompliant(false)]` with a documented reason.
3. Keep non-compliant members out of new public API going forward — the
   attribute turns the compiler into the enforcer.

## AVOID
- Do not slap `[assembly: CLSCompliant(true)]` on while leaving
  non-compliant public members unmarked and their warnings unaddressed —
  the attribute then *lies* to consumers, which is worse than not declaring.
- Do not mark individual members compliant while the assembly stays
  unmarked; the assembly-level declaration is what the rule (and consumers)
  look for.
- Do not suppress with `#pragma warning disable` or `[SuppressMessage]`.
  Suppression of analyzer diagnostics is forbidden by the project's AGENTS.md.
